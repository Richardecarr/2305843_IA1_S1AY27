Personality

My top three traits and hobbies

1. Leadership.

What it involves: Leadership means guiding a group toward a goal. It includes planning, dividing the work, communicating clearly, listening to others and taking responsibility for the result. I practise it as President of the UTech Jamaica Tech Hub, where I help plan workshops and student programmes.

Why it is important: Most work, at school and in a job, is done in teams. A team without direction wastes time, and a good leader helps everyone contribute and finish on time.

Impact on my school life: In group projects I am comfortable organising tasks and keeping the team on track. It also gives me confidence to speak up in class and ask questions.

2. Being responsible

What it involves: Being responsible means doing what I said I would do, being honest, and paying attention to details. As treasurer of three student groups, I kept financial records and stuck to budgets.

Why it is important: People depend on me, and small mistakes with money or deadlines can cause big problems. Being reliable builds trust, which matters in school, at work and in leadership roles.

Impact on my school life: I plan ahead, hand in work on time, and check my work before I submit it. It also helps me balance classes, my job and club activities.

3. Solving problems

What it involves: Problem solving means finding out why something is not working and fixing it step by step. In my IT jobs I troubleshot hardware, software and network problems, and building projects like my chess game meant finding and fixing errors in my code.

Why it is important: Things break in technology all the time. Staying calm and working through a problem is what separates a person who gives up from one who finishes the job.

Impact on my school life: When my code has errors in labs, I test it one piece at a time until I find the cause. It keeps me patient, and I learn more from fixing a mistake than from avoiding it.

My hero: Usain Bolt

Background Usain Bolt was born in 1986 in Sherwood Content, a small community in Trelawny, Jamaica. He grew up playing cricket and football before his school coaches noticed how fast he was. He went on to become a sprinter, and he won eight Olympic gold medals. In 2009 he set the world records in the 100 metres (9.58 seconds) and the 200 metres (19.19 seconds), and those records still stand. He retired from racing in 2017.

My favourite moment is the 100 metres final at the 2008 Beijing Olympics. Bolt was so far ahead that he slowed down and started celebrating before the finish line, and he still broke the world record. To me it shows what happens when you prepare so well that you can trust yourself when it counts.

I look up to Bolt because he came from a small town and became the best in the world, and he never stopped representing Jamaica with pride. He worked very hard in training even though he made it look easy. He also stayed relaxed and confident under huge pressure, and he still made time to have fun. I try to do the same in school: prepare properly, stay calm when the deadline or exam comes, and keep my sense of humour. His story shows me that where you start does not decide where you finish.

LLMs: a tool, not a crutch

To me, using an LLM as a tool means I stay in charge of the thinking and the AI helps me do it better. Using it as a crutch means I let it think for me. UNESCO warns that we may become too dependent on generative AI and lose our human agency, which could hurt the development of intellectual skills (Miao & Holmes, 2023).

As a computer science student, I agree with this. If an AI writes my code every time, I might pass the lab, but I never learn how to program. World Economic Forum

I try to use LLMs in ways that help me learn. When I am studying, I can ask it to explain a concept in a simpler way or give me examples, like how a SQL join works. When my code has an error, I can ask what the error message means and then fix the problem myself. I can also ask it to quiz me before a test or check my English after I have written something. In all of these cases, I do the work first and use the AI second.

There are also times when I should not use it. I should not use it to write assignments that are supposed to show my own ideas, like my reflection on a book. That would be cheating, and the work would not be mine. I also should not trust everything it says, because an LLM can be wrong and still sound sure. I check its answers against my notes, my textbook and my lecturers, and I follow my course rules about using AI.

A simple test I use is this: could I explain the answer to someone else without the AI? If yes, I learned something. If not, I was leaning on a crutch. Used this way, an LLM is like a tutor who is available at any time, but I am still the one who has to do the learning.

Sources: Miao, F., & Holmes, W. (2023). Guidance for generative AI in education and research. UNESCO. https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/guidance-generative-ai-education-and-research
